# Squabblemon — Season One story bible

Revision: 2026-09-08. Editorial canon for the revised screenplay. Supersedes conflicting worked examples. Authored story is not automatically deployed game content.

## The promise

A newcomer wins a neighborhood card tournament just as its missing champion comes home. Keeping the courts open means getting between two brothers who have spent four years telling different stories about the same night.

The season asks: **When you say you did it for the family, who actually paid?**

Street comedy gives the neighborhood life. Family drama gives victories their cost. Matches settle tournament positions, public challenges, and agreed wagers. They cannot prove paternity, authenticate recordings, cure illness, or force forgiveness.

## The player

The player is a newcomer with a small crew and a deck. Do not prescribe gender, family, criminal history, or a voiced avatar. They enter the Block Party Open because it promises anyone a place to play, then discover Blue and Red have made access depend on wearing a side's colors.

Winning grants the ceremonial Block Crown and the right to set the next open-play schedule. It grants no property ownership. The player competes to keep slots open to unaffiliated crews. Their success makes them an independent witness and a threat to both brothers' control.

Announced tournament terms and NPC responses establish this motivation. Stage directions may show the player putting down a deck or holding ground. No branching choice system is required. NPCs remain responsible for their own decisions.

## Tone — heightened neighborhood comedy and family melodrama

Big entrances. Public arguments. Petty authority. An envelope that makes everybody sit back down. The block should feel like the cookout, the group chat, the church parking lot, and a championship broadcast have all booked the same folding table. Play the ridiculous events sincerely: nobody on this block thinks their own behavior is the joke.

Let characters roast, interrupt, exaggerate, curse, defend the indefensible, and drag an argument somewhere embarrassingly specific. Use conversational contractions and street slang where that speaker would use them. Do not give everybody the same vocabulary. The comedy is in their different minds: one person makes a threat, another asks who paid for the microphone.

Every scene gets a comic situation, an escalation, and a visual payoff. Blue builds a throne from folding chairs; Wifey makes him carry it. Cornball investigates a vending machine and wins sparkling water. Snitch sells an exclusive entrance that arrives with a low-battery warning. Baby Momma unplugs the ring light and turns a coronation into an accountability hearing.

Blue is a self-appointed neighborhood executive who cannot survive cross-examination by his wife. Red is precise enough to make a correction sound like a threat. Wifey punctures grandeur with where the power cord actually goes. Snitch treats every disaster as a subscription opportunity. Cornball makes an everyday inconvenience a federal case. Alley Runner delivers dry answers into somebody else's full production. Cracked Head speaks like the entrance music is still playing. Baby Momma can commandeer a rooftop with one sentence and a chair.

Jokes can sit beside hurt. Blue's memorial-shirt rant is both outrageous and real grief: play the anger, not an impression of someone being funny. After a joke exposes a wound, let the reaction land. Baby Momma's family decisions carry weight even when her insults bring the house down. Escalate the presentation without changing who knows a secret or making a game result prove a family allegation.

Keep spoken cards readable, usually one or two short sentences. Put spectacle and physical business in stage directions. Every required encounter changes access, allegiance, knowledge, or responsibility; optional scenes add texture and never hold the only essential clue.

## Four years ago — writers only

Cracked Head arranged a warehouse handoff of stolen electronics. Blue gave Snitch the location, hoping public embarrassment would displace his brother. He knew exposing the location was dangerous; he did it anyway. He did not intend a death.

Snitch sold the location onward. OG Uncle learned that the exposed handoff threatened his eldest son's life and warned the authorities. Cracked Head survived, cooperated against the organizers, and entered a fictional protective relocation arrangement. Do not present real witness-protection procedure.

OG Uncle let the block believe Cracked Head had died. There was a memorial, not a recovered body. He could have said the death was unconfirmed; he chose a lie that stopped questions. Baby Momma was pregnant and was left to grieve and raise their child. There is no second pregnancy this season.

During his absence, Cracked Head learned he had a child. He sent an early message through Red asking after them, then used safety to justify silence. The threat explains his departure; it does not excuse every year. Red delivered practical support but concealed its source and the message.

The present-day season takes roughly six weeks. The immediate threat has passed. Cracked Head chooses a public tournament appearance because he would rather arrive as a champion than knock on Baby Momma's door as an absent father.

| Chapter | New information and consequence |

| 1 | Cracked Head is alive and Blue's brother. Blue is stunned; Red is not. Baby Momma demands privacy. |
| 2 | Cracked Head and Baby Momma have a child. Red concealed a message sent after the memorial. |
| 3 | OG Uncle is seriously ill and maintained the death story. Blue finally visits. |
| 4 | The viral recording is edited. Snitch sold the handoff address and names Blue as its source. |
| 5 | OG Uncle explains the rescue. Blue admits his motive. Each owns a distinct harm. |
| 6 | Cracked Head wants Blue publicly displaced. Blue signs shared-access terms, then prepares to break them. |
| 7 | Blue altered the contender list to exclude his brother. He restores it and accepts disqualification, losing his own title shot. |
| 8 | The rooftop owner has signed a sale agreement. Tournament authority is not ownership. |

Cracked Head is physically present in Chapter 1. Never stage his survival as a new discovery afterward. Chapter 7's return means returning to formal competition to reclaim authority.

## Ending locked for this draft

OG Uncle lives through Chapter 8. Illness supplies neither a countdown nor a magical recovery. Blue confesses, sacrifices his title shot, and begins earning trust. Cracked Head loses the final to the player and honors the result. Baby Momma agrees to a specific first parenting commitment, not an automatic reunion.

Church Auntie hosts a shared meal. No compulsory wedding or funeral. The rooftop owner confirms a sale agreement with Techbro Rich. Cracked Head cannot sell property he never owned. The neighborhood keeps its community and faces a new threat to where it gathers.
